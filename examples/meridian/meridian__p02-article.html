Senate passes landmark AI safety bill in 72–34 vote

The bipartisan measure introduces mandatory pre-release evaluations, an incident registry, and new whistleblower protections — but stops short of the licensing regime industry critics had feared.

EM
By Elena Marquez Senior policy correspondent · Updated 14 minutes ago

The Capitol after Thursday's vote. The bill is the first comprehensive federal AI legislation passed by either chamber. Photo: Meridian / Andrew Liu

WASHINGTON — After three days of floor debate and a flurry of last-minute amendments, the Senate on Thursday passed the Frontier AI Accountability Act in a 72–34 vote, sending the most consequential American technology regulation in two decades to the House next week.

The bill requires developers of frontier models — defined as systems trained with more than 10²⁶ floating-point operations — to conduct pre-deployment safety evaluations against a standardized federal benchmark, publish redacted results, and notify the new Office of AI Oversight within 72 hours of any incident causing material harm.

It also creates a federal incident registry, modeled in part on the National Transportation Safety Board, and expands whistleblower protections to contractors and red-team researchers working on frontier systems.

"This isn't about slowing things down. It's about not flying blind. If a 747 has to be tested before it carries passengers, a model that drafts our medical advice and our legal contracts can be tested too."

— Sen. Margaret Holst (D-WA), the bill's lead author

Industry response was more measured than many on Capitol Hill had expected. The four largest U.S. AI labs — all of whom had opposed earlier drafts — issued a joint statement on Thursday afternoon calling the final text "workable," citing the removal of a pre-training licensing requirement and the addition of a 180-day safe harbor for good-faith disclosures.

What's in the final bill

Three provisions drew particular attention in the final hours of debate. The first, an amendment from Sen. Reyes (R-TX), exempts open-source models below a 50-billion-parameter threshold — a carveout that civil society groups had pushed for and that several open-weights labs had treated as a redline.

The second establishes the federal incident registry, which will require labs to file standardized post-incident reports within 30 days and authorizes the Office of AI Oversight to publish anonymized summaries quarterly. The third, which survived a near-tie vote, extends the federal whistleblower statute to cover internal safety teams and external red-team contractors.

Notably absent from the final text is any requirement for pre-training licensing, which had appeared in the bill's January draft and drawn sharp opposition from researchers and open-source advocates. A proposal to require third-party audits of training data also failed in committee.

What happens next

The bill's path through the House is less certain. Speaker Caldwell has not committed to a floor vote, and at least 40 House Republicans have signed a letter calling for hearings before any action. The administration has signaled it will sign the bill if it reaches the Resolute desk unchanged; senior White House officials told Meridian they consider the text "a meaningful but minimum first step."

Beyond Washington, the international response is already taking shape. The European Commission welcomed the vote in a brief statement, noting "substantial alignment" with the bloc's existing AI Act. Officials in Tokyo and Singapore have signaled they will move quickly to publish compatible frameworks.

"It's not perfect — no first-in-the-world law ever is," said Dr. Kavya Iyer, director of the Center for AI Policy at Georgetown. "But it sets the table. The hard work now is on the agencies that have to implement it, and on Congress to keep funding them while they do."
